Artificial Intelligence, Worker Reallocation, and Occupational Networks

Sebastian Frånlund Johansson

Abstract

Artificial intelligence reaches occupations unevenly, and how exposed workers fare depends on which of their tasks remain with labor and on which jobs they can reach. I construct a complementarity-adjusted dynamic AI exposure index, C-DAIOE, that discounts technical exposure in the task contexts where human input remains valuable, and link it to Norwegian employer–employee registers for 2010–2024 and to an occupational mobility network estimated from pre-period worker transitions. Within firms, a one-standard-deviation increase in exposure is associated with 11 percent lower employment in an occupation, but only where task complementarity is low; raw exposure has no relationship with employment. The decline operates through fewer hires into exposed occupations rather than more exits from them. Across workers, direct exposure is associated with more occupation and employer switching, whereas exposure among the occupations a worker could move to is associated with less, and movers are more likely to enter less exposed, more complementary, and better-paid occupations, the more so when the destinations their occupation historically fed into are themselves less exposed. Exposure is not associated with short-run earnings growth.

Lost in Transition? Job Displacement and Industry Earnings Premiums in the Petroleum Sector

with Jon Ellingsen and Caroline Espegren

Abstract

We study earnings losses among workers displaced from a high-premium petroleum industry. Using Norwegian matched employer-employee data, we follow workers displaced during the mass layoffs after the 2014 oil-price collapse. Combining coarsened-exact matching with an interaction-weighted event-study design, we decompose earnings losses using AKM-based industry premiums. Four years after displacement, earnings are approximately 14 percent below those of comparable non-displaced workers. We document that the petroleum industry has the highest estimated earnings premium in the economy and that movement to lower-premium industries accounts for approximately 38 percent of the four-year earnings loss. Moreover, institutional arrangements shape how earnings losses translate into total-income losses. Public transfers partly cushion the decline in earnings but do not fully offset the resulting loss in total income. The findings highlight a labor-market channel of transition risk: sudden contractions in high-premium fossil-fuel industries may deprive displaced workers of access to sector-specific earnings premiums that are difficult to replace elsewhere.

Working paper

The Impact of Labor Demand Shocks on Disability Benefit Uptake

with Espen R. Moen

Abstract

This paper studies the effect of local labor demand shocks on disability benefits and the uptake of vocational rehabilitation, exploiting the 2014 global oil price collapse as a shock to the Norwegian labor market. Leveraging detailed administrative data and employing an event study design alongside a shift-share instrument based on municipal exposure to oil-sector employment, we document increases in both disability benefits and vocational rehabilitation following the shock. Our 2SLS estimates imply negative elasticities of disability and vocational rehabilitation enrollment with respect to local labor market conditions. Using local wages as our preferred proxy for labor demand, a one percent increase in wages reduces disability enrollment by 0.757 percent and vocational rehabilitation enrollment by 2.029 percent. Instrumenting local employment instead yields elasticities of the same sign, −0.769 and −1.070 respectively, though the employment channel is less precisely identified. Our findings suggest that local labor market conditions are an important driver of social insurance caseloads in a comprehensive, generous welfare state such as Norway's.
